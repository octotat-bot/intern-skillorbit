<!-- chunk id=bp-001 | title=Contact header essentials | topic=contact | roles=all | sections=contact | source=self-authored -->
The header should contain your full name on the first line, then a professional email, a phone number with country code, a LinkedIn URL and a GitHub or portfolio URL, separated by vertical bars on one or two lines. Write URLs as plain text (linkedin.com/in/your-name) so they survive copy-paste and ATS parsing even when hyperlinks are lost. Do not include photos, date of birth, marital status or full postal address; city and country are enough.

<!-- chunk id=bp-002 | title=Professional email and profiles | topic=contact | roles=all | sections=contact | source=self-authored -->
Use an email address based on your name, for example firstname.lastname@gmail.com, rather than nicknames or birth years. Customize your LinkedIn URL so it reads cleanly. Your GitHub profile should pin your two to four best repositories, each with a README describing the problem, the tech stack and how to run it, because reviewers often open the first pinned repository.

<!-- chunk id=bp-003 | title=Writing a short summary | topic=summary | roles=all | sections=structure,quality | source=self-authored -->
A summary is two to three lines at the top that state your target role, your strongest skills and one concrete achievement. Example: "Final-year CS student targeting data analyst roles; skilled in SQL, Python and Power BI; built a sales dashboard used by 3 teams during an internship." Avoid generic objectives such as "seeking a challenging position in a reputed company"; they say nothing specific about you.

<!-- chunk id=bp-004 | title=Section order for students | topic=structure | roles=all | sections=structure | source=self-authored -->
For students and fresh graduates a reliable order is: header, summary, education, skills, experience or internships, projects, certifications, achievements. Put experience before projects once you have a relevant internship. Use standard headings ("Experience", "Projects", "Skills") rather than creative titles, because both recruiters and ATS look for familiar labels.

<!-- chunk id=bp-005 | title=Strong bullet formula | topic=bullets | roles=all | sections=projects,experience,quality | source=self-authored -->
Write each bullet as Action + Method + Result. Start with a strong past-tense verb, say how you did it with which tools, and end with a measurable outcome. Weak: "Worked on a website for a client." Strong: "Built a responsive React storefront for a local retailer, cutting page load time from 4s to 1.5s." Keep bullets to one or two lines.

<!-- chunk id=bp-006 | title=Action verbs that show ownership | topic=verbs | roles=all | sections=projects,experience,quality | source=self-authored -->
Open bullets with verbs that show what you personally did: Built, Designed, Developed, Implemented, Automated, Optimized, Deployed, Analyzed, Led, Reduced, Increased. Avoid weak openers like "Helped", "Worked on", "Was responsible for" or "Participated in", which hide your contribution. Vary verbs so three bullets in a row do not all start with "Built".

<!-- chunk id=bp-007 | title=Quantifying impact as a student | topic=metrics | roles=all | sections=projects,experience | source=self-authored -->
Numbers make claims credible. Even student work can be quantified: dataset size (50,000 rows), model accuracy (89%), performance (2x faster, 40% smaller bundle), users (used by 120 classmates), time saved (6 hours a week), scale (handles 1,000 requests per minute) or scope (3 modules, 4-person team). If you do not know an exact figure, measure it now or give a defensible estimate; never invent numbers.

<!-- chunk id=bp-008 | title=Describing projects | topic=projects | roles=all | sections=projects | source=self-authored -->
Give each project a title line with the tech stack, for example "Churn Predictor | Python, scikit-learn, Flask", followed by two or three bullets: what problem it solves, how you built it, and the result or scale. Link the repository or live demo. Two strong, finished projects relevant to the target role beat six tutorial clones.

<!-- chunk id=bp-009 | title=Choosing which projects to show | topic=projects | roles=all | sections=projects | source=self-authored -->
Pick three to five projects that match the role you are applying for, and order them by relevance rather than date. Prefer projects with real users, real data, deployment, or a measurable result. Group-project bullets should state your own contribution ("Designed the database schema and REST API") instead of the team's.

<!-- chunk id=bp-010 | title=Organizing the skills section | topic=skills | roles=all | sections=skills | source=self-authored -->
Group skills into labelled lines such as "Languages: Python, SQL, Java", "Frameworks: React, Flask", "Tools: Git, Docker, Tableau" and "Databases: PostgreSQL, MongoDB". Ten to twenty-five specific skills is a healthy range. List only skills you could discuss in an interview, and use the exact names employers use (PostgreSQL, not "Postgre").

<!-- chunk id=bp-011 | title=Proving the skills you list | topic=skills | roles=all | sections=skills,projects,experience | source=self-authored -->
A skill listed only in the skills section is a claim; the same skill named in a project or internship bullet is evidence. For each key skill, make sure at least one bullet shows where you used it. Soft skills such as "teamwork" are better demonstrated in bullets ("Coordinated a 4-person team...") than listed.

<!-- chunk id=bp-012 | title=Education entry details | topic=education | roles=all | sections=education | source=self-authored -->
Each education entry should state the degree and major (B.Tech in Computer Science and Engineering), the full institution name, the graduation year or expected graduation year, and your CGPA or percentage if it is 7/10, 70% or higher. Relevant coursework (Data Structures, DBMS, Machine Learning) can be added in one line for freshers. School results can be dropped once you are in university unless asked.

<!-- chunk id=bp-013 | title=Certifications that add value | topic=certifications | roles=all | sections=structure,skills | source=self-authored -->
Certifications help most when they fill a gap your projects do not cover, for example a cloud practitioner certificate for a cloud role or a data analytics certificate for an analyst role. List the issuer and year. Many reputable courses are free or low cost. Do not list every short online course; three relevant ones are enough.

<!-- chunk id=bp-014 | title=Achievements and extracurriculars | topic=achievements | roles=all | sections=structure | source=self-authored -->
An achievements section can include hackathon results, coding contest ratings, scholarships, publications, open-source contributions and leadership roles in technical clubs. Quantify where possible ("Top 5 of 300 teams", "Codeforces rating 1650"). Keep hobbies out unless they are directly relevant.

<!-- chunk id=bp-015 | title=Ideal resume length | topic=length | roles=all | sections=quality | source=self-authored -->
Students and early-career candidates should fit the resume on one page, roughly 300 to 600 words. A resume under 150 words looks incomplete; one over two pages is rarely read fully. Cut content that is old, irrelevant to the target role, or repeated, rather than shrinking the font below 10 points.

<!-- chunk id=bp-016 | title=Avoiding first person and filler | topic=tone | roles=all | sections=quality | source=self-authored -->
Resumes are written in an implied first person: "Built a dashboard", not "I built a dashboard". Remove pronouns such as I, my and me. Replace clichés like "hard worker", "team player", "passionate" or "results-driven" with evidence: a bullet showing results proves drive better than the word itself.

<!-- chunk id=bp-017 | title=Consistent formatting | topic=formatting | roles=all | sections=quality | source=self-authored -->
Use one bullet symbol, one date format (for example "Jun 2024 - Aug 2024"), one font family, and consistent spacing and capitalization throughout. Align dates in the same place for every entry. Small inconsistencies signal carelessness to reviewers even when the content is strong.

<!-- chunk id=bp-018 | title=Describing internships | topic=experience | roles=all | sections=experience | source=self-authored -->
For each internship give the title, company, location (or Remote) and dates on one line, then two to four bullets on what you delivered. Focus on outcomes ("Automated weekly reporting, saving the team 6 hours a week") rather than duties ("Responsible for reports"). Teaching assistant roles, research assistantships and freelance work also count as experience.

<!-- chunk id=bp-019 | title=Tailoring to each application | topic=tailoring | roles=all | sections=skills,quality,ats | source=self-authored -->
Tailor the resume for each role: reorder projects so the most relevant come first, mirror the exact terms used in the job description where they are true for you, and move the matching skills to the front of each skills line. Keep a master resume with everything, and produce a focused one-page version per application.
